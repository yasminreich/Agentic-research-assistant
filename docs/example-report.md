## Scientific Summary

RESEARCH QUESTION: Does colostrum fat matter?

SHORT ANSWER: Yes. Although colostrum research has historically been dominated by immunoglobulin G (IgG) and passive immunity, the accumulated evidence indicates that colostral fat matters as a concentrated, rapidly available energy source for thermoregulation and metabolic adaptation of the neonate, as a carrier of bioactive lipids and lipid-soluble factors, and as a distinctive, developmentally tuned lipid fraction. Its importance is clearest for energy/thermoregulation and neonatal survival (especially under cold or resource-limited conditions), while its role relative to IgG in overall passive-immunity outcomes is secondary. Several gaps and some disagreement remain.

1. COLOSTRUM IS FAT-RICH AND ITS LIPID FRACTION IS COMPOSITIONALLY DISTINCT
Colostrum is characterized by an elevated fat content compared with mature milk, and the fat fraction is not merely "more of the same." In bovine colostrum, palmitic (C16:0) and myristic (C14:0) acids are higher, whereas short-chain fatty acids (C4:0, C6:0) are markedly lower than in mature milk; the specific fatty-acid profile of colostrum is interpreted as reflecting a biological requirement of the neonatal calf, and it differs by parity and milking number (Fatty acid profile characterization in colostrum, transition milk, and mature milk — W4206670865). Fat globule size roughly doubles across the first postpartum milkings, with the fourth milking being compositionally distinct, indicating a rapidly changing lipid structure during the colostrum-to-milk transition (Bovine colostrum: postpartum changes in fat globule size and fatty acid profile — W4214928603). In humans, colostrum is enriched in high-molecular-weight and unsaturated triacylglycerols (e.g., OPO/OPL, 18:1/18:1/16:0) relative to mature milk, which contains more medium-chain fatty acids; these structured lipids are linked to improved digestion, absorption and immune development in infants (Differences in triacylglycerol and fatty acid compositions of human colostrum and mature milk — W2799725810; Triacylglycerol composition of breast milk during different lactation stages — W2914655244). Reviews of colostrum composition confirm fat is a major nutritional component across species and is a determinant of colostrum energy value (Importance of colostrum in the newborn dairy calf — W4210744513; Implications of placentation type on species-specific colostrum properties — W4294127631; Nutritional and management factors that influence colostrum production and composition — W4391043144).

2. COLOSTRAL FAT MATTERS FOR ENERGY, THERMOREGULATION AND METABOLIC ADAPTATION
The strongest recent, direct evidence comes from a controlled calf study in which calves received either full-fat (4.81% fat) or low-fat (0.58% fat) colostrum from the SAME pooled batch during the cold season: colostral fat content modulated glucose homeostasis and lipid metabolism during postnatal metabolic adaptation, demonstrating that the fat component per se — independent of IgG and other constituents — influences how the neonate adapts metabolically to cold (Colostral fat content modulates glucose homeostasis and lipid metabolism in neonatal calves exposed to cold temperatures — W7148701318). Consistent with this, feeding a 2-fold upconcentrated colostrum replacer (roughly doubling fat, protein and lactose) altered systemic metabolism (glucose, NEFA and lipid metabolites) as well as immunity and fecal profile relative to native colostrum (Feeding 2-fold upconcentrated versus native colostrum — W7162990691). Across ruminants, the importance of colostrum-derived energy for thermoregulation and survival is well established: in lambs and kids, achieving rapid colostrum intake provides fuel for thermoregulation and is crucial for neonatal survival, and maternal energy nutrition governs colostrum supply (Improving neonatal survival in small ruminants — W1879107293; Colostrum production in ewes: regulation and energy supply — W1986340919). In piglets, colostrum energy intake is central to thermostability, growth and survival, particularly in large litters and low-birth-weight/IUGR piglets (Improving the performance of neonatal piglets — W3201476633). Colostrum intake also drives circulating adiponectin and broad metabolic/endocrine activation in neonatal calves, underscoring the metabolic (not solely immune) role of the first meal (Rapid increase of circulating adiponectin depends on colostrum intake — W2150894696).

3. COLOSTRUM FAT AS A CARRIER OF BIOACTIVE AND GROWTH-PROMOTING FACTORS
Beyond energy, colostral lipids carry and accompany bioactive substances that promote gastrointestinal maturation. Colostrum's growth-promoting effects on the neonatal calf gut are mediated by lipid-associated and lipophilic bioactives (e.g., cholesterol carriers, hormones, IGF) interacting with intestinal receptors and receptor-like transporters (Growth-promoting effects of colostrum in calves — W2257718622). Dietary fatty acids more generally shape gut barrier function, mucosal immunity and microbial ecology in young animals; medium-chain fatty acids are absorbed directly and fuel enterocytes while exerting antibacterial effects, illustrating why the fatty-acid make-up of the first feeds is functionally relevant (Effects of dietary fatty acids on gut health and function of pigs — W3014009126). n-3 fatty acid supply also has immune and developmental relevance in cattle (Roles of dietary n-3 fatty acids in dairy cattle — W2885487370).

4. FAT VARIES AND CAN BE MANAGED, AND EXTENDED FAT-RICH (TRANSITION) MILK FEEDING HELPS
Colostral fat content varies considerably among cows and is influenced by maternal factors and prepartum nutrition. Dry-period dietary energy level alters colostrum fatty-acid composition (Effect of dry period dietary energy level on colostrum IgG, insulin and fatty acid composition — W2219130798), and specific supplements (e.g., prepartum beta-carotene) can raise colostral fat and protein concentrations (Effect of beta-carotene supplementation to prepartum cows — W3163461690). Practically, extending feeding of fat-rich colostrum/transition milk improves outcomes: partially replacing whole milk with colostrum for 2 weeks improved growth and reduced diarrhea and pneumonia (Extended colostrum feeding for 2 weeks — W3043335290), and extended transition-milk feeding for 3 weeks improved growth and reduced diarrhea (Extended transition milk feeding for 3 weeks — W3114124577). Reviews emphasize that colostrum supplies both nutrient (including fat/energy) and non-nutrient factors that support growth, intestinal maturation and health (Importance of colostrum supply and milk feeding intensity — W3004735230; New frontiers in calf and heifer nutrition — W3165415339).

5. IMPORTANT CAVEATS, DISAGREEMENTS AND GAPS
(a) For the classic endpoint of passive immunity, the dominant determinants are IgG concentration, volume fed and timing — not fat; the major colostrum-quality frameworks and on-farm tools (e.g., Brix) center on IgG/total solids, with fat measured as a covariate rather than a primary driver of transfer of passive immunity (Importance of colostrum in the newborn dairy calf — W4210744513; Brix refractometry across bovine, caprine, ovine colostrum — W3108122357; MIR spectroscopy for colostrum composition — W4384937183). (b) Some evidence shows other components can be limiting independent of fat: adding IgG from a whey-based (low-fat/low-casein) replacer to low-quality colostrum achieved adequate serum IgG and absorption, indicating IgG mass, not fat, drove passive transfer in that setting (Comparison of IgG absorption in calves fed maternal colostrum, colostrum replacer, or supplemented colostrum — W3012003149). (c) The single most direct causal test isolating colostral fat (W7148701318) and the upconcentration study (W7162990691) used small numbers of calves (n=12; ~6 per group) and short observation windows, so effects on longer-term health, mortality and production remain to be quantified. (d) Much of the fat-specific metabolic/thermoregulation evidence is strongest for cold or energy-limited conditions and for species with high neonatal mortality (piglets, lambs); the marginal value of extra colostral fat under thermoneutral, well-managed conditions is less clear. (e) The venue filter (high-impact journals only) excluded numerous smaller-journal papers (e.g., in Animals, Nutrients, Frontiers), so some applied evidence is not represented here.

CONCLUSION: Colostrum fat does matter. It is a compositionally distinct, energy-dense fraction that supports thermoregulation and metabolic adaptation of the newborn, carries growth- and gut-maturation-promoting bioactives, and is manageable through maternal nutrition and extended transition-milk feeding — with the clearest benefits under cold or energy-challenged conditions. However, for the specific outcome of transfer of passive immunity, IgG quantity and timing remain the primary drivers, and the direct causal evidence isolating colostral fat is still limited in scale and duration.
